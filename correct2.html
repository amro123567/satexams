Scaling: lengths k, areas k², volumes k³

7 min

Missed on Exam 1 M2 Q17 (similar cones) and Full SAT M2 Q20 (similar triangles). Both times the length ratio was used for an area or a volume.

When two figures are similar, every length in one is the same multiple k of the matching length in the other. That multiple is the scale factor.

Area multiplies two lengths (base × height), so it grows by k × k. Volume multiplies three lengths, so it grows by k × k × k.

The units are the shortcut: cm is one length, so k; cm² is two lengths, so k²; cm³ is three lengths, so k³.

What you measureUnitsScale byIf k = 3
Side, height, radius, perimetercmk× 3
Area, surface areacm²k²× 9
Volumecm³k³× 27

Rule

Find k from two matching lengths. Given an area ratio, take the square root first to get k. Given a volume ratio, take the cube root. Then raise k to the power the question needs.
Worked example (Exam 1 M2 Q17) Similar cones have surface areas in the ratio 9 : 16, and the larger volume is \(128\pi\). Lengths: \(\sqrt{9}:\sqrt{16}=3:4\). Volumes: \(3^3:4^3=27:64\). Smaller volume \(=128\pi\cdot\frac{27}{64}=54\pi\).

Traps

  • Using k on an area or volume (the most common wrong choice on the SAT).
  • Using an area ratio as if it were a length ratio, without taking the square root.
  • Using the leftover piece instead of the whole side, such as DB instead of AB in a triangle with a parallel line.

Try it

Two similar cones have heights 4 and 10. The smaller cone's volume is 24. What is the larger cone's volume?
375. k = 10/4 = 2.5, and volume grows by 2.5³ = 15.625. 24 × 15.625 = 375.
Two similar rectangles have areas 50 and 200. The smaller one is 5 wide. How wide is the larger one?
10. Area ratio 4, so k = √4 = 2, and 5 × 2 = 10.
2

Circles: arcs, sectors and radians

6 min

Left blank on Exam 1 M1 Q20 (sector area with radians); missed on Full SAT M2 Q16 (arc length, where the diameter was used as the radius).

An arc and a sector are both a fraction of the whole circle. The fraction is the central angle divided by a full turn: \(\frac{\theta}{360^\circ}\), or \(\frac{\theta}{2\pi}\) in radians.

  • Arc = that fraction × circumference \(2\pi r\)
  • Sector = that fraction × area \(\pi r^2\)

Radians: 180° = π. One radian is the angle whose arc equals the radius.

FindDegreesRadians (θ in radians)
Arc length\(\frac{\theta}{360}\times2\pi r\)\(s=r\theta\)
Sector area\(\frac{\theta}{360}\times\pi r^2\)\(A=\tfrac12r^2\theta=\tfrac12rs\)
Convertdegrees × \(\frac{\pi}{180}\) = radians; radians × \(\frac{180}{\pi}\) = degrees

Rule

First write down the radius. From \(C=36\pi\): \(2\pi r=36\pi\), so r = 18 (36 is the diameter). From area \(81\pi\): r = 9. Then use the fraction of the circle.

Traps

  • Using the diameter in a formula that needs the radius (gives double the arc).
  • Giving the sector area when the question asks for the arc length, or the reverse.
  • Using \(s=r\theta\) with θ in degrees: it only works in radians.

Try it

A circle has radius 10. A central angle measures \(\frac{3\pi}{5}\) radians. Find the arc length and the sector area.
Arc 6π, sector 30π. \(s=10\cdot\frac{3\pi}{5}=6\pi\). \(A=\tfrac12\cdot10\cdot6\pi=30\pi\).
A circle has a circumference of 24π. What is the length of an arc with a central angle of 150°?
10π. \(\frac{150}{360}=\frac{5}{12}\), and \(\frac{5}{12}\times24\pi=10\pi\). No radius needed.
3

Quadratics: three forms, symmetry, and one solution

8 min

Advanced Math is where most points went: Exam 1 M1 Q5, M1 Q22, M2 Q10 and Full SAT M2 Q17, M2 Q19. The calculations were fine; what was missing was reading the structure.

FormShows directly
\(ax^2+bx+c\)y-intercept c; opens up if a > 0
\(a(x-p)(x-q)\)zeros p and q
\(a(x-h)^2+k\)vertex (h, k): the min or max is k

Rules

  • Axis of symmetry: \(x=-\frac{b}{2a}\), which is also the midpoint of the zeros, \(\frac{p+q}{2}\).
  • Symmetry: if two different inputs m and n give \(f(m)=f(n)\), then their midpoint \(\frac{m+n}{2}\) is the axis.
  • Completing the square: take half of b, square it, then add and subtract it. \(x^2-8x+7=(x-4)^2-9\).
  • Exactly one solution (or a line touching the parabola once): the discriminant \(b^2-4ac=0\), and the solution is \(x=-\frac{b}{2a}\).

Traps

  • Answering with the constant k (or c) when the question asks for x. Read the last line again.
  • Picking factored form when the question asks for the minimum or maximum.
  • The sign in vertex form: \((x-4)^2\) has its vertex at x = 4, not −4.
  • \(f(2x)\) moves points horizontally (x-values are halved) but doesn't change the minimum value.

Try it

f(x) = (x + 2)(x − 6). What is the vertex of the graph of f?
(2, −16). Axis = (−2 + 6)/2 = 2, and f(2) = (4)(−4) = −16.
x² − 6x + k = 0 has exactly one real solution. What is the solution?
x = 3. One solution means it's a perfect square: (x − 3)², so k = 9, but the question asks for x.
f(x) = x² + 4x + 1. If f(m) = f(m + 10), what is m?
m = −7. Axis x = −2. m and m + 10 sit 5 on each side of it: −7 and 3.

Exponential rates: make the exponent just t

6 min

Both rate questions were missed on Exam 1 (M1 Q19 with \(t/6\), M2 Q21 with \(2t\)). He got this right on the full SAT, so this is a quick reinforcement.

In \(A(t)=a\cdot b^t\), the base b is the factor per one unit of t. If the exponent is \(t/6\) or \(2t\) instead of plain t, the base you see is not the yearly (or hourly) factor yet.

ModelRewriteFactor per unitRate per unit
\(240(0.5)^{t/6}\)\(240\left(0.5^{1/6}\right)^t\)0.89111% decrease
\(2000(1.03)^{2t}\)\(2000\left(1.03^{2}\right)^t\)1.06096.09% increase

Rule

Rewrite \(b^{t/n}=\left(b^{1/n}\right)^t\) or \(b^{nt}=\left(b^{n}\right)^t\). Compute the new base: above 1 means growth of (base − 1), below 1 means decay of (1 − base).

Traps

  • Reading the rate per 6 hours (or per half-year) as the rate per hour (or per year).
  • Dividing the percent, as in 50% ÷ 6: exponential change isn't linear.
  • Giving the amount that remains (89%) instead of the amount that decays (11%).

Try it

P(t) = 400(1.21)^(t/2), with t in years. By what percent does P grow each year?
10%. \(1.21^{1/2}=1.1\).
V(t) = 900(0.5)^(t/3), with t in years. About what percent does V lose each year?
About 20.6%. \(0.5^{1/3}\approx0.794\), and 1 − 0.794 ≈ 0.206.
A(t) = 100(1.05)^(4t), with t in years. What is the yearly growth rate?
About 21.55%. \(1.05^{4}\approx1.2155\).

Checking habits (27–30 min)

These three habits would have saved most of the points that weren't concept gaps.

  1. Re-read the last line before choosing. Ask: is my number what they asked for? (x or k? per year or per 2 years? AB or DB? arc or sector?)
  2. Plug the answer back in. Ten seconds in the original equation caught 4 of the 7 full SAT errors. With > or <, test the boundary value.
  3. Never leave a blank, and pace for a check. There's no penalty for guessing. Aim to finish each Math module about 5 minutes early to check your answers.
